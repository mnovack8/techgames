Insight Rhetoric

Trust as a
Compounding Asset

Credibility is built through dozens of small consistent actions and destroyed by one. Aristotle understood this 2,400 years ago. Most technical professionals are still learning it the hard way.

11 min read
← Back to Blog

There is a category of technically excellent engineer who consistently gets passed over for leadership roles, whose proposals get more scrutiny than those of less rigorous colleagues, and whose correct recommendations are frequently ignored until someone else makes the same recommendation later. The work is not the problem. The work is often exceptional. The problem is that the work is being evaluated by people who have not yet decided to trust the person making it, and no amount of additional technical rigor fixes that.

The Work-Speaks-for-Itself Assumption

Most engineers carry a model of professional credibility that goes something like this: produce high-quality work consistently, and over time people will recognize it. The recognition is assumed to follow naturally from the quality, the way a passing test follows from correct code. The relationship is direct and the mechanism is automatic.

This model is wrong in a specific way. It confuses the quality of the output with the credibility of the source, and it assumes the second follows automatically from the first. In practice, the credibility of the source is evaluated separately from the quality of the work, often before the work is even examined, and the evaluation uses entirely different criteria.

Aristotle identified this 2,400 years ago. In his Rhetoric, he described three modes of persuasion available to any speaker: logos (the argument itself), pathos (the emotional connection with the audience), and ethos (the credibility and character of the speaker). His observation was that ethos is the most powerful of the three, not because character matters more than logic, but because audiences use the character of the speaker to decide how much weight to give the argument before they evaluate it. Logos without ethos does not fail because it is wrong. It fails because it does not land.

λόγος
Logos
The argument itself: the data, the reasoning, the evidence, the logic of the case.
Where technical professionals spend almost all their energy.
πάθος
Pathos
The emotional resonance: whether the audience feels heard, whether the stakes feel real to them.
Often dismissed as manipulation. Actually: does the audience care?
ἦθος
Ethos
The credibility of the speaker: track record, character, demonstrated competence and goodwill.
The prerequisite. Logos cannot land without it. This is what compounds.

The engineering translation: a technically perfect argument from a source with low ethos will be discounted, scrutinized more heavily, or attributed to luck when it proves correct. The same argument from a source with high ethos will be accepted faster, given more benefit of the doubt, and remembered as evidence of the source's judgment. The argument did not change. What changed is whether people were primed to receive it.

What Ethos Is Actually Made Of

The question rhetoric answers
Why does the same argument land differently from different people, and what can you do about it?

Aristotle broke ethos into three components, each of which contributes differently to whether an audience trusts a speaker. All three are active in every professional relationship, whether the people involved are conscious of them or not.

φρόνησις
Phronesis
Practical wisdom
The demonstrated ability to make sound judgments in real situations. This is not theoretical knowledge. It is track record. Every time you called something correctly, delivered on a commitment, or navigated a difficult situation well, you added to your phronesis in the eyes of the people who observed it. Every time your estimate was wrong, your recommendation failed, or your delivery fell short, you reduced it.
How it shows: your past calls were right. When you said it would take three weeks, it took three weeks.
ἀρετή
Arete
Virtue and character
The sense that you act from principle rather than convenience. Arete is revealed in difficult moments: whether you delivered the honest assessment when the honest assessment was uncomfortable, whether you took responsibility when something went wrong, whether your behavior in adversity matched your stated values. It cannot be performed in easy moments and withheld in hard ones. Audiences notice the gap.
How it shows: you said the hard thing when it would have been easier not to. You owned the miss.
εὔνοια
Eunoia
Goodwill toward the audience
The perception that you are acting in the interests of the people you are addressing, not just your own. This is the component technical professionals most consistently neglect. A recommendation that is technically correct but transparently self-serving (it is the approach that plays to your strengths, the solution that gives your team the most interesting work) will be received with less trust than one that clearly considered the audience's interests, even if the conclusion is identical.
How it shows: your recommendations are perceived as serving the problem, not your preferences.

The practical point: ethos is not a single thing you either have or don't. It is three separate accounts, each maintained by different behaviors, each observable by the people around you independently. You can have high phronesis and low eunoia (technically respected, politically distrusted). You can have high eunoia and low phronesis (well-liked, not listened to on hard calls). The full picture requires all three.

Trust as a Compounding Asset

The question rhetoric answers
Why does credibility grow slowly but disappear fast, and how is it actually accumulated?

The compounding analogy is precise, not poetic. Like financial compounding, trust grows through consistent small deposits over time, and the rate of return increases the higher the balance. A person with high accumulated credibility gets the benefit of the doubt on new claims, has their past successes remembered when new proposals are evaluated, and recovers more easily from the occasional miss. A person with low accumulated credibility gets the opposite treatment at every step.

What makes trust a compounding asset rather than a simple accumulation is the interest effect: high trust reduces the friction on all subsequent influence attempts. You do not have to re-establish credibility at the start of each conversation. The accumulated balance carries forward.

The Trust Ledger: Deposits and Withdrawals
Deposits
Delivering on a commitment by the date you said
Flagging a problem before it becomes visible to others
Saying "I don't know" when you don't know
Giving credit clearly and specifically to the right person
Giving honest feedback when it costs you something
Owning a miss without deflecting to context
Following through on a small thing no one was tracking
Recommending against your own interests when it's the right call
Withdrawals
Missing a deadline you committed to without early warning
Overclaiming confidence and being wrong
Blaming circumstances for a miss that was within your control
Taking credit visibly for shared work
Recommending the solution that suits you, framed as if it's neutral
Saying one thing in the meeting and a different thing outside it
Dropping a small commitment because no one reminded you
Being right loudly in a way that makes the other person wrong publicly
Note the asymmetry: deposits are small and frequent. Withdrawals are larger per transaction and more memorable. The ledger is not balanced by volume.

The asymmetry is the critical structural feature. Trust is not built and destroyed at the same rate. Research in organizational behavior and social psychology consistently finds that negative information about a person's reliability is weighted more heavily than positive information of the same magnitude. One visible broken commitment does not undo one kept commitment. It can undo many.

The rhetorical tradition understood this intuitively. Aristotle noted that a speaker who has been caught in one lie loses credibility far beyond the specific claim that was false, because the audience can no longer calibrate which claims to trust. The same dynamic operates in professional relationships: a single broken commitment does not just reduce the trust balance. It raises the question of whether the entire prior balance was real.

The Follow-Through Problem

The question rhetoric answers
Why do small commitments matter so much more than they seem to?

Every commitment you make is a liability on your credibility account until it is fulfilled. This includes small ones: the follow-up email you said you'd send, the document you said you'd review, the question you said you'd look into. These are not trivial. They are the texture of phronesis in practice, the repeated small evidence from which people extrapolate to larger judgments.

The common engineering mistake is treating small commitments as low-stakes because the stakes of the specific task are low. The task might be low-stakes. The commitment is not. A colleague who observes you drop three small follow-throughs in a month will not give you the benefit of the doubt on the large one that matters. Not because they are being irrational, but because they are being Bayesian: small consistent evidence is more predictive of future behavior than stated intentions.

The commitment trap
The easiest way to make a commitment is to say yes to something in the moment to avoid the discomfort of saying no. The cheapest commitment feels free when you make it. It costs credibility when you miss it.
The rhetorical fix
Make fewer commitments and fulfill them completely, rather than more commitments fulfilled partially. A smaller set of promises delivered exactly is worth more to your ethos account than a larger set of promises delivered mostly.

This has a practical implication for how you handle overcommitment when it happens, and it will happen. The rhetorical literature on credibility repair is clear on one point: early acknowledgment of a likely miss preserves far more trust than a missed deadline with a post-hoc explanation. "I said I'd have this by Thursday. I can now see that's not realistic, and I wanted to tell you before Thursday" is a deposit. A missed Thursday with an email Friday morning is a withdrawal. The content of the two messages is nearly identical. The timing is everything.

Why Logos Alone Fails

The question rhetoric answers
Why do correct arguments get ignored, and what is actually being evaluated when a recommendation lands or doesn't?

The logos-only approach to professional credibility produces a specific failure mode that engineers encounter and frequently misread. Your proposal is technically superior. You have the data. You have modeled the alternatives. The recommendation is clearly right. And yet the decision goes the other way, or gets delayed indefinitely, or the same recommendation is accepted three months later from someone else. You conclude that the organization is irrational or political.

Sometimes that is true. Often it is not. What is usually happening is that the audience evaluated your ethos before they evaluated your argument, and the ethos evaluation came back insufficient. Not because your technical judgment is wrong, but because they do not yet have enough data on you as a source to accept a significant recommendation on the basis of your analysis alone. The argument requires trust as a precondition. The trust is not there. The argument does not land.

What the engineer thinks happened
My recommendation was technically correct and well-evidenced. The decision-makers were irrational, politically motivated, or did not understand the analysis.
↓
What usually happened
The decision-makers did not have enough accumulated evidence about this person's judgment to stake a significant decision on their recommendation alone. They waited for corroboration, a second voice, or a track record that did not yet exist. The argument was evaluated through the filter of unestablished ethos, and did not pass.

The solution is not to produce a better argument. It is to invest in ethos before the argument needs to land. This is a sequencing insight: credibility must be accumulated in advance of the moment you need it, through the small consistent deposits that the trust ledger above describes. You cannot build ethos at the moment of a high-stakes proposal by making the argument more rigorous. You build it in the dozens of low-stakes interactions that precede it.

Aristotle's phrasing was more direct: "We believe good men more fully and more readily than others." The modern translation is blunt: people do not evaluate arguments in a vacuum. They evaluate arguments from people. The person is assessed first, and the argument is processed through that assessment. This is not irrational. It is a reasonable heuristic for managing the cognitive cost of evaluating every claim from scratch. It just has consequences for anyone who has not understood that their character is being assessed continuously, not only when they are in the room and presenting.

Building Ethos Deliberately

The question rhetoric answers
What specific behaviors accumulate credibility, and which ones spend it without the person realizing?

Ethos is not personality and it is not likability. It is a record, assembled from observable behaviors over time, and it can be managed deliberately. The three Aristotelian components point to three different behavioral domains, each requiring a different kind of attention.

To build phronesis (track record)
Size your commitments to what you can actually deliver, not what you wish you could. An accurate small estimate delivered on time is worth more to phronesis than an ambitious estimate missed by a week.
When you make a prediction, note it. When the outcome arrives, compare. Over time you will find your systematic biases (most engineers underestimate time and overestimate the speed of organizational decisions) and correct for them publicly.
Say "I don't know" clearly and specifically. "I don't know, but I'll find out by Wednesday" is a phronesis deposit twice over: accurate calibration plus a kept follow-through commitment.
To build arete (character)
Deliver the honest assessment even when it is uncomfortable, but choose the moment. Honesty volunteered at the wrong time reads as recklessness. Honesty delivered thoughtfully in a one-on-one before a public meeting reads as integrity.
When something goes wrong on your watch, own it before someone else names it. The public acknowledgment of a miss is a significant ethos deposit, because it demonstrates that your credibility is not contingent on your record being clean.
Be consistent whether or not you think you are being observed. Arete is not about performance; it is about what you do when there is no audience. Word travels. People compare notes. The gap between public and private behavior is eventually visible.
To build eunoia (demonstrated goodwill)
Before making a recommendation, make the other person's perspective visible in the argument: "I know the concern has been timeline, so here is how this affects that." This signals that you considered their interests, not just the technical problem.
Give credit specifically and publicly. "This approach came from a conversation with Jana, who pointed out the edge case I had missed" costs nothing and signals that your recommendations are oriented toward the best outcome, not toward your own visibility.
Occasionally recommend against your own interests explicitly. "The faster path here is the one your team owns, not mine." Done sparingly and genuinely, this is the highest-value eunoia deposit available. It demonstrates that your recommendations can be trusted because they are not self-serving by construction.

The Long Game

Ethos compounds. The engineer who has built ten years of consistent phronesis, arete, and eunoia across an organization does not have to argue as hard for each new proposal. The trust balance carries the argument partway before the logos even starts. The inverse is also true: the engineer who has spent a decade producing high-quality work while treating credibility as an automatic byproduct has a technically impressive portfolio and an ethos account that does not reflect it, because they were never managing the account.

Aristotle's observation was that the most effective communicators are not the ones with the strongest arguments. They are the ones whose audience has already decided to trust them before the argument begins. That trust was not given. It was accumulated, one small consistent action at a time, long before the moment it was needed.

The Practical Summary
Your credibility is being evaluated continuously, not only when you are presenting. Every small commitment kept or dropped, every honest assessment given or withheld, every credit given or claimed, every recommendation made in the interest of the problem or the interest of your position: these are all entries in a ledger others are keeping about you. The work does not speak for itself. You speak for yourself through all of it, all the time. Managing that deliberately is not politics. It is the application of a 2,400-year-old insight to the professional relationships that determine whether your work matters.
