The Compounding Return
on Human Skills

Technical skills depreciate. The framework you mastered three years ago is already losing value. The judgment you built over the same period is worth more today than when you started. Aristotle understood why. Most engineers never make the switch.

9 min read
← Back to Blog

There is a pattern in how technically strong engineers think about their own development. They track the technical skills they are acquiring the way they track tools in a project: what they know, what they still need to learn, where the gaps are. The model is essentially a skills inventory. Maintain the inventory, add to it when needed, and career trajectory takes care of itself. This model is not wrong. It is incomplete in a way that becomes increasingly costly over time.

Two Kinds of Knowledge

Aristotle drew a distinction that has not been improved on in 2,400 years. He separated knowledge into several categories, but two are directly relevant here: techne and phronesis.

Techne is craft knowledge — the mastery of a specific method or tool for producing a specific outcome. Carpentry is techne. Writing SQL is techne. Configuring a Kubernetes cluster is techne. It is learnable, teachable, and inherently tied to the domain in which it applies. A carpenter's techne does not transfer to surgery. A Java developer's techne does not transfer automatically to Rust. The knowledge is real and valuable, but it is local to a context.

Phronesis is practical wisdom — the capacity for sound judgment in complex, ambiguous, real-world situations. It is not a collection of facts or techniques. It is the ability to perceive what a situation actually requires, weigh competing considerations, and act well. Aristotle was explicit that phronesis cannot be taught in the way techne can. It can only be developed through practice, experience, and reflection over time. And unlike techne, it is not local to a domain. The person with well-developed phronesis brings it to every situation they encounter.

Compounds. Each situation handled well adds to the reserve. The person with twenty years of deliberate practice is categorically better than the person with two, in a way that has no parallel in technical knowledge.

The investment implication is direct: technical skills are a depreciating asset that requires continuous maintenance to retain value. Human skills are a compounding asset that returns more the longer they are held and practiced. A portfolio weighted entirely toward techne will look strong at year five and show cracks by year fifteen. A portfolio that also builds phronesis will outperform it substantially over any long time horizon.

The Depreciation Model

The question philosophy answers
Why does expertise in a specific tool not translate into lasting career advantage, and what kind of knowledge actually does?

Technical knowledge depreciates for a structural reason: it is tied to a specific tool, method, or paradigm, and those things change. This is not a criticism of technical skills. It is simply how domain-specific knowledge works. The same mechanism that makes techne learnable (it is concrete, specific, and testable) also makes it replaceable. When the domain shifts, a new techne is required.

The Technical Skill Lifecycle
Emerging
High value, scarce supply. Early adopters who invested in this skill command a premium.
Growth
High value, growing supply. Certification programs launch. Bootcamps offer courses. Premium erodes.
Mature
Table stakes. Everyone hiring for this role expects this skill. It no longer differentiates. It merely qualifies.
Declining
The tool or paradigm is being superseded. Maintaining the skill costs more than the return it earns.
Every technical skill you have today is somewhere on this curve. Most are already past peak differential value.

This is not an argument against learning technical skills. It is an argument against treating them as the primary investment. The person who spent the last twenty years maintaining only a technical skill inventory has had to run continuously just to stay current. The person who spent the same period also developing phronesis has an asset that has been appreciating the entire time, independent of which tools were fashionable in any given year.

The Compounding Model

The question philosophy answers
What makes human skills different from technical skills in the way they accumulate, and why can't they be learned on demand?

Aristotle was specific about why phronesis cannot be acquired the way techne can. Techne can be transmitted: a skilled carpenter can teach an apprentice the techniques of the craft. Phronesis cannot be transmitted in the same way because it is not a set of techniques. It is a disposition — a trained capacity to perceive situations accurately and respond to them well. That capacity is only developed by being in situations and responding to them, repeatedly, over time, with enough reflection to learn from the outcomes.

The Stoics extended this with the concept of prokope — progressive moral and practical development. The Stoic idea was that the person who has been practicing right reasoning and right action for twenty years is not just someone who has accumulated more facts than the person who started last year. They have developed a different kind of faculty. The older practitioner perceives situations the newer one cannot yet perceive, not because they have memorized more, but because their capacity for perception has been trained by years of deliberate practice.

Technical value peaks early and requires maintenance to prevent decay. Human skill value grows slowly, then accelerates. The crossover is around year 8 to 10 for most engineering careers.

The compounding mechanism works like this: every time you navigate a difficult conversation well, you develop a slightly more accurate model of how people respond to difficult conversations. Every time you make a decision under uncertainty and reflect on the outcome, your capacity for that kind of decision improves. The improvement is not dramatic from one instance to the next. But it is cumulative, and it does not depreciate. The judgment you developed ten years ago in a different role, with different technology and a different team, is still available to you today. The specific technology stack you mastered in that same role probably is not.

The Asymmetry You Cannot Correct by Cramming

The question philosophy answers
Why does starting late on human skills cost more than starting late on technical skills, and what is actually lost by waiting?

The most consequential feature of the techne/phronesis distinction is not that one appreciates and one depreciates. It is the time structure of the investment. Technical skills can be acquired relatively quickly when demand emerges. If a new framework becomes important, a motivated engineer can reach proficiency in months. The market responds to demand. Training programs, documentation, and communities of practice scale quickly.

Phronesis cannot be accelerated in the same way. There is no bootcamp for practical wisdom. You cannot compress twenty years of judgment development into six months by reading more intensively. The compounding requires time as an input, not just effort. The person who starts developing these skills at twenty-five has ten more years of compounding by thirty-five than the person who starts at thirty-five. That gap does not close.

Technical skill gap
Can be closed. A motivated person can reach proficiency in a new language, framework, or tool within months. The market responds to demand with training resources that scale. Starting late costs time, not trajectory.
Closeable on demand
Human skill gap
Cannot be fully closed. Practical wisdom requires time as an input, not just effort. The engineer who has been practicing deliberate reflection, difficult communication, and sound judgment for ten years has a compounding advantage that cannot be replicated by intensity alone.
The gap is permanent

This is not a pessimistic point. Starting now is always better than starting later. The compounding begins the moment you make the investment. But it is an argument against treating human skills as something to address later, after the technical foundations are solid. The optimal strategy is to invest in both continuously from the beginning, because the cost of delay in one category is recoverable and the cost of delay in the other is not.

What This Means in Practice

The question philosophy answers
If human skills compound through practice and reflection, what specifically should an engineer be practicing, and how do you measure progress?

Aristotle's answer to this was behavioral: phronesis is developed by doing what a person of practical wisdom would do, repeatedly, until it becomes second nature. The modern translation requires being specific about which capacities to develop and how to create deliberate practice opportunities for each of them.

Judgment under uncertainty
Make explicit predictions. Note your confidence level. Track outcomes and compare. Over time your calibration improves and your systematic biases become visible and correctable. This is not a soft skill. It is a trainable faculty that depreciates only when you stop practicing it.
Explored in: Deciding Under Uncertainty
Trust and credibility
Track the commitments you make and whether you fulfill them. Identify patterns in when you over-commit and why. Build the habits that make your credibility account grow rather than erode. This is the slowest-building and longest-lasting of the human skills.
Explored in: Trust as a Compounding Asset
Systems thinking about people
Develop the habit of modeling human systems the way you model technical ones: inputs, outputs, feedback loops, failure modes. The engineer who can do this fluently operates effectively at a scale that technical skill alone cannot reach.
Explored in: An Engineering Approach to Human Systems
Communication and influence
Notice how different framings of the same information land differently with different audiences. Build an explicit model of how the people you work with process and respond to arguments. This model improves with every interaction you treat as data.
Explored in: Authority vs. Influence

The measurement problem is real. Technical skills produce visible outputs: a working system, a passing test, a deployed feature. Human skill development is harder to see in the short term. The feedback loops are longer and the signal is noisier. This is part of why engineers underinvest in it: the return is not visible on the timescale most people use to evaluate whether a skill investment is paying off.

Aristotle's answer to the measurement problem was eudaimonia — a life well-lived, assessed over the whole arc of a career rather than any particular quarter. The engineer who has been building both kinds of capital for twenty years will have a very different career by the end of it than the one who built only techne. The comparison is not visible at year three. It is unmistakable at year fifteen.

The Investment Case
You cannot time this market. There is no signal that will tell you when to shift from technical investment to human skill investment. The optimal strategy is continuous investment in both, starting now, because the compounding on human skills requires time as its primary input. Every year you delay the start is a year of compounding you cannot recover. Every year you invest is a year that pays returns for the rest of your career, independent of which technologies rise and fall in the meantime.
