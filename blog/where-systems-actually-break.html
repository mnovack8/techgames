Competence Is Local.
Coherence Is Designed.

Every other framework on this blog is pointing at the same idea from a different angle. This is that idea, named directly, and a map of where to go for the long version of each piece.

7 min read · July 1, 2026
← Back to Blog

A security analyst who understands the vulnerability perfectly cannot get engineering to prioritize the fix. A workshop teaches AI literacy in one module and cybersecurity in another, and participants leave with two separate skills instead of one useful one. A bike-share program buys good bikes and writes clear rules, and the bikes still end up broken. A brilliant engineer tops out at senior while a less technical peer gets promoted past them. Four different failures, in four different domains, and underneath all of them, the same story.

The Pattern: Systems Fail at the Seams, Not the Centers

None of the four situations above failed because a part was missing. The analyst was competent. The workshop content was accurate. The bikes were well made. The engineer was genuinely skilled. In every case, the pieces were fine. What failed was the connection between them: the interaction layer that was supposed to carry intent from one side to the other and didn't.

That's the whole theory, and it's short enough to say in one line: competence is local, coherence is designed. Being good at your part of a system is necessary. It has never been sufficient. What determines whether the parts add up to something that works is whether someone deliberately built the connective tissue between them, and that's a different job from getting good at any one part.

Three domains. Three seams. One diagnosis: the parts on either side were fine, and the thing that needed deliberate design was the interface between them.

Why the Fix Is Never "More Expertise"

The instinctive response to a seam failure is to reinforce one of the sides. If engineering won't prioritize the fix, train the analyst to write a better ticket. If the workshop feels disjointed, add more slides on AI fundamentals. If the bikes keep breaking, buy sturdier bikes. Every one of these responses adds depth to a component that was never the problem, and every one of them fails for the same reason: depth on one side of a seam does not build the seam.

The seam is interaction-layer work. It's the risk conversation that translates a CVE into a business decision. It's the workshop arc that makes the security exercise depend on the AI concept taught twenty minutes earlier. It's the accountability structure that ties a damaged bike back to the rider who caused it. None of that is a component you can buy more of. It has to be designed, on purpose, by someone who can see both sides of the gap at once.

The Reward Is Real. It's Just Illegible.

There's a fair objection to all of this: if seam-building is so valuable, why doesn't everyone do it? The honest answer is that most organizations do reward it, just not with a straight line anyone can point to. Performance systems are tuned to reward what's easy to count: tickets closed, sprints shipped, models trained. Seam-building pays out too, in promotions, in who gets invited into the room, in who gets asked to lead, but that payout arrives later, attaches to a person rather than a sprint, and never shows up as a line item on a review.

Given a legible, immediate reward for depth and a real but delayed, hard-to-quantify reward for building the seam, most people rationally default to the reward they can see. That's not a personal failing and it's not an organizational failure to care. It's an incentive-design problem, the same one Agents, Components, and Interactions describes at the level of whole systems: agents respond to the incentives that are actually visible to them, not the ones that are technically in place. The fix isn't asking people to work against their incentives through willpower. It's building enough personal fluency in the other side that your own incentives shift, which is the same move the STEM / Humanities Bridge argues for from the vocabulary seam.

💡
Learning a bit about the other side doesn't just add a skill. It changes what you notice and what you want, which changes how you show up in the interaction. That's an agent-level fix, not a component-level one, and it's the reason individual fluency-building actually works even when the org's formal incentives are still fuzzy.

Where to Start

Whatever seam you're looking at, whether it's between departments, disciplines, or the agents and incentives inside a single system, the same three moves apply.

Diagnose the seam, not the parts. Before improving either side, ask what's supposed to pass between them, and what actually does. Most of the diagnostic work in every piece on this blog happens right here, before anyone touches a fix.

Build fluency at the agent level first. A little real understanding of the other side changes what you value, not just what you know. That shift is durable in a way that a one-time process fix isn't, because it travels with the person instead of living in a document nobody rereads.

Make sure the interaction layer reinforces it. Individual fluency-building is fragile if the system around it still only rewards the old, narrow behavior. The bridge holds when the people who build it are also the people the system visibly rewards for building it, even if that reward is slower and fuzzier than the metrics everyone's used to watching.

None of the pieces on this blog are really about bikes, or board games, or AI workshops, or CVEs. They're about this: the seams are where systems actually break, competence on either side of a seam was never going to be enough, and building the connective layer on purpose is the highest-leverage work available, in a career or in a system, precisely because almost nobody is doing it deliberately.
