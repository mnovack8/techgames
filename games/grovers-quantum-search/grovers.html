How to Play — Grover's Quantum Search

The Goal

Working as a team, identify the hidden quantum number (0–255). Each player holds one secret clue. If you could see all clues at once, they would point to exactly one number. You cannot speak — only act.

Before You Start

Hand the Quantum Token to any player your team chooses. That player goes first and breaks ties at the end.

Your Clue

Your private clue is a property of numbers 0–255. It is true for the target number, but also true for many others. Use the "All Possible Clues" reference to see example gate cards that match or don't match a clue, and reason about other players' YES/NO votes.

Gate Cards

You start with 3 gate cards in your hand. Each card shows a number (0–255) and its 8-bit binary form. No talking.

On Your Turn

  1. Choose a gate card from your hand and play it face-up.
  2. Place your token in YES if that number matches your clue, or NO if it doesn't. (This happens automatically in the digital version.)
  3. Every other player's token goes in YES or NO based on whether their own clue matches that number. The digital version works this out for you — you just confirm it.
  4. Draw a new card from the deck to refill your hand to 3.

The Central Board

At any time during the game you may move your two tokens on the shared central board:

  • Confidence: ?, Low, Medium, High — signal how sure you are that your team knows the answer.
  • Number Estimate: Narrow your guess from a quarter (0–63 etc.), to a tenth, to an exact number. Moving your token here tells teammates what number you think is the target.

After 12 Rounds

The game enters the Measurement Phase. Each player votes for the exact number they believe is the target. The most-voted number is the team's answer. If there is a tie, the player holding the Quantum Token decides.

Correct: team wins. Wrong: team loses. Choose wisely.

Quantum Concept

This game models Grover's search algorithm — YES votes constructively interfere (reinforce the correct answer), NO votes destructively interfere (eliminate wrong ones), and the final measurement collapses all probability into a single guess, just as in a real quantum circuit.
